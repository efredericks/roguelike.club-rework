## When and Where?

Roguelike Celebration 2026 will be held on Saturday and Sunday **October 17 -- October 18, 2026** as a **virtual event** in our custom-built multiplayer game/chat space. Talks will also be streamed to Twitch and YouTube.

Our call for presenters closed on July 19, 2026. We will respond to all submissions by mid-August.

 There will also be a free preview event **September 13, 2026** in the same social space, with talks streamed to Twitch and YouTube:

* "Ancient Roguelikes: The Pre-History of Roguelike Games" by Martin Pichlmair
* "Bitwise Black Magic: How to Computer, Better Faster Stronger" by Greg L
* "Don't Save The @: What Roguelikes Teach Us About Story Structure" by Jamie Brittain

Check back here for more details and a schedule as the event approaches.

| **15:15** | 22:15 | `T` | **Don't Save The @: What Roguelikes Teach Us About Story Structure**<br><sub>As someone who has worked in tv, film - and now AAA gaming, I have long been obsessed with story structures, both as a tool to use in my own work, but also as perhaps the biggest grift in the creative industries today. *The Hero With A Thousand Faces*, Robert McKee's Story, Dan Harmon's 'story wheel', and perhaps most of all, Blake Snyder's 'Save The Cat' all contain much wisdom, but they have also contributed to the mass homogenisation of storytelling across culture. How many times have you been reading a book, watching a movie, playing a game, and have found that you know precisely what is going to happen next, and after that, and after that...<br><br>I would like to look at how the stories we experience in roguelike games resist the predictability of story structure orthodoxy. Permadeath, random level generation, ascii graphics and turn based gameplay could all be viewed as antithetical to a gripping narrative structure, but, as anyone who has played one of these games knows, they can create some of the most immersive and emotional experiences we can have with games. I will argue that roguelikes teach us about the importance of avoiding well travelled paths (and structures) when we tell stories.</sub> | Jamie Brittain |
